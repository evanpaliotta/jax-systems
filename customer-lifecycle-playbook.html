Jax Systems: Customer Lifecycle Playbook

Operating draft v2 · 14 November 2026 · One manual, first enquiry through clean exit. For Evan to run the business, not customer-facing marketing copy. This reading copy is publicly accessible: blank templates and fictional examples only. Keep actual prospect lists, client records, agreements, incident logs and credentials in an access-controlled system, never here.

The objective: understand the business, deliver a useful Business Improvement Plan, sell only work that can be bounded and supported, launch safely, and know whether it keeps working. AI is optional. Prefer a simpler process or an existing product feature when it solves the problem.

What this is—and is not: a researched operating design with executable checklists and templates. It does not install a CRM, create email infrastructure, run monitoring, establish a support rota, authorize outreach, or prove any client result. The fictional rehearsal in section 16 tests the reasoning, not a production system. Read section 17 before selling or activating anything.

1. How to use this manual

Use one private client record throughout the lifecycle. Each stage below states its trigger, inputs, responsible people, procedure, output, advance gate and failure path. Complete a gate with evidence, not a tick based on confidence. Mark a record ready, blocked, paused, not proceeding or complete; never quietly skip a failed gate.

Current offer: resolve the old-document conflict

The live pricing page was checked for this revision. It now specifies a free working session on one of seven business areas, for one specific question, up to 60 minutes, with the owner or decision-maker present. The session uses client-owned tools, stays read-only unless a change is approved, may include one small safe reversible fix, ends with a short written summary, and does not include customer sends or recording without consent.[1]

The paid paths are now explicit. Path A is a $2,499 first standard build for one agreed improvement, and it starts only after a signed scope card covering the trigger, end state, tools, rules, exclusions, tests, owner and price.[1] Path B is a $999 Business Improvement Plan that reviews all seven areas at a high level, ranks opportunities, sets measurement, and delivers one implementation blueprint the client owns.[1]

The plan is credited once: $2,499 total minus $999 already paid = $1,500 remaining, not $3,498 combined.[1] Eligible additional improvements are $1,500 when they reuse the approved setup and access, and eligible additional support is $250/month only when the extra improvement is live and genuinely reuses the approved setup and access.[1] Optional support for the first agreed improvement remains $499/month.[1] Software/provider charges are separate, the client keeps the plan without buying a build, and build handoff is not conditional on buying care.[1]

Older “Ops X-ray,” pricing-range and free-scoping discussions in the workspace are historical. This manual supersedes their conflicting operating guidance; it does not rewrite past research or signed terms. Recheck the published offer and signed agreement before each quote. The contract governs that engagement; escalate any mismatch instead of silently changing either promise.

Who is accountable

  • Evan / engagement owner: qualification, commercial decisions, delivery accountability, capacity, client communication and support coordination. One person may wear several hats; that does not create independent coverage.
  • Client sponsor: budget and business priorities; signs the agreement or identifies the authorized signatory.
  • Client workflow owner: supplies real examples, validates rules, runs acceptance checks and owns manual fallback.
  • Client system administrator: grants/revokes access and authorizes technical changes within their authority.
  • Named reviewer or specialist, where needed: verifies risky decisions or work beyond Evan's capability. Do not invent an available security team, subcontractor or backup responder.
  • AI assistance: drafts, analysis and bounded tests only within approved tools/data permissions. It cannot sign, spend, contact clients, expand scope or authorize production changes on its own.

Decision hierarchy: signed engagement terms → current approved commercial decisions → this manual → historical source ideas. Applicable law and binding provider rules still apply. Resolve contradictions before proceeding; a proposal, verbal interest or dashboard is not a contract.

Standard handoff line: client ID; stage; evidence links; decision and decision-maker; next action; owner; due date/time zone; blocker; permission status. Never store a password or token in that line.

2. The lifecycle and its advance gates

The journey is linear enough to run, but permits a no, a pause, a return to discovery or an exit. A second sales meeting is optional, not a mandatory persuasion ritual.

A gate can only be waived by someone with authority, with the reason, risk, compensating control and expiry recorded. Do not waive unauthorized access, false claims, missing consent or an unsafe external action merely to preserve a deadline.

3. Attract and qualify: website, buyer list and email

Trigger: an enquiry arrives, a referral is offered, or Evan activates a specific acquisition experiment. Inputs: current offer, target hypotheses, public-source evidence, permitted channels, private record system and available response/delivery capacity. Owner: Evan. Output: a small, source-backed queue of conversations, not an indiscriminate contact dump.

Select a buyer without pretending demand is validated

Start in Jacksonville / Northeast Florida with an owner or operating decision-maker who has recurring work, a measurable consequence, and someone who can adopt a change. Keep a broad service portfolio, but test one buyer/problem combination in an individual outreach batch. Possible signals: the owner describes missed handoffs, repeated entry, ageing quotes or inconsistent follow-up. Public reviews, hiring and website gaps are research signals—not proof that a business is broken or wants help.

Before a record becomes contact-ready, verify: still operating; actual business versus branch/location; plausible decision route; dated source for the contact channel; relevance; provider/legal eligibility; suppression status. Publicly listed contact information is not a consent record. Do not import personal addresses, sensitive records or unverified inferred emails just to fill a column.

Prospect-record template: prospect ID; business/location identity; geography; observed signal + source URL/date; problem hypothesis; buyer role and evidence; public business contact source/date; last verification; legitimate contact basis; jurisdiction/provider-policy check; suppression status; fit/risk; stage; next action/owner/date. Missing information stays unknown. Private research exports do not become outreach-ready automatically.

Disqualifiers or stop-and-review: no credible problem; unwilling sponsor; request for guaranteed revenue; need for round-the-clock critical coverage Jax cannot provide; unclear permission; regulated/high-consequence work beyond capability; prohibited provider use; no workable fallback; project economics that require hidden unpaid support. Declining a bad fit protects both sides.

Email infrastructure: a readiness gate, not a warm-up ritual

Do not buy inboxes or activate warming from this document. At activation:

  1. Select a provider whose acceptable-use terms permit the proposed activity. Establish the real sender identity, domain strategy, mailbox owner, reply handling and budget; do not use lookalike identities or rotate domains to evade filters.
  2. Configure and verify SPF, DKIM and DMARC as the Jax baseline, with forward/reverse DNS and TLS checked through the provider where applicable. Gmail's minimum authentication requirements distinguish all senders from bulk senders; do not mistake a bulk threshold for permission to send below it.[3]
  3. Verify a real test from the intended sending path to controlled Gmail and another provider: authentication results, readable content, received inbox/spam location, working Reply-To, actual reply receipt, bounce capture and opt-out handling. A provider's “sent” status is not a receipt.
  4. Confirm applicable commercial-email requirements and provider rules before a campaign, including truthful identity/subject, required advertising disclosure, a valid postal address, an easy opt-out and suppression. The FTC guide requires accurate routing information and non-deceptive subjects; commercial versus transactional classification depends on message purpose, not the sender's label.[4] Obtain qualified legal advice for the actual audience/jurisdictions; this checklist is not a complete legal opinion.
  5. Test suppression across every sending tool before the first real batch. Jax's proposed operating policy is immediate suppression on opt-out, no further promotional follow-up; retain only the restricted suppression evidence needed to honor it. Never put an opted-out lead back into a fresh import.
  6. Start with an explicitly approved, small batch and a named person to read replies. Inspect delivery errors, complaints, negative replies and fit before expanding. Google advises against purchased lists and sudden volume spikes; warming is not proof of permission or guaranteed delivery.[3]

Keep business-critical enquiry/support delivery separate from acquisition activity where practical, without pretending subdomains provide perfect reputation isolation. After any MX/SPF/DKIM change, retest the existing website notification and reply path; do not break inbound enquiries while setting up outbound.

Outreach and follow-up policy—proposed, not activated

Lead with an observed fact, label the hypothesis and ask a low-pressure question. No fake case study, assumed loss calculation, disguised customer enquiry or invented familiarity. Example structure: “I noticed [verifiable fact]. Does [specific recurring handoff] cause extra work for your team, or have you already got that handled?” Add the required commercial footer and opt-out when applicable.

For an activated first pilot, propose one initial message and at most two relevant follow-ups across roughly two working weeks, unless the person asks for a different timing. Stop on opt-out, hard bounce, complaint, a clear no, or evidence of wrong person; record the reason. An auto-reply is not interest. Route positive replies to a human and stop the sequence; respect an out-of-office return date rather than adding touches blindly. Silence is not consent to continue indefinitely.

Advance gate: sending path tested, compliance/provider check complete, suppression tested, list verified, claims and batch authorized, response owner available. Failure path: pause that channel, preserve diagnostics, correct the cause and retest. Do not compensate for poor delivery by sending more.

4. Handle the enquiry and run the free working session

Trigger: a real response, website enquiry or referral. Inputs: original message/source, current offer, minimal public context and scheduling capacity. Owner: Evan. Output: fit decision, concise notes, a short written summary and one mutually agreed next step. Proposed internal target: check the enquiry queue each working day and acknowledge within one working day when available; this is not a published SLA.

Deduplicate without losing the source. Record when it arrived, what the person asked, who will reply and the next due date. Confirm the website notification actually reached the queue. Never treat form submission as a newsletter subscription. Ask permission before recording/transcribing; follow applicable recording law, explain tool/storage use, and use written notes if permission is absent or unclear.

Free-session outline (up to 60 minutes)

  • 0–5 minutes: frame the boundary. “We’ll stay with one question in one business area, use your own tools, keep access read-only unless you approve a change, and finish with a short written summary. If one safe next step is clear, we’ll say so. If the scope is still unclear, I’ll recommend the Business Improvement Plan.”
  • 5–20: walk one recent example. “Show me the last time this happened.” What starts it? Where does information enter? Who checks it? Where does it wait or get repeated? What already works?
  • 20–35: inspect the bottleneck. Confirm owners, rules, exceptions, records and risk. Keep the session at the current-state and practical-next-step level; do not drift into a free business-wide diagnostic.
  • 35–50: test the next step. If a small change is safe, reversible and approved, make it together in the client-owned tool. Do not send customer messages, spend money or widen scope without explicit authority.
  • 50–60: summarize and route. Record what was reviewed, what was observed or measured, what changed if anything, the next recommendation, and whether the right next step is Path A or Path B.

Fit and note template

Record: source/contact permission; business area and question; recent example and actors/tools; frequency; observed versus owner-estimated impact; what works/prior attempts; current provider/native alternatives; decision-maker and workflow owner; sensitivities; what stayed read-only versus what changed; fit / uncertain / not fit with reason; written summary delivered; agreed next step, owner and date.

Fit for Path A: recurring meaningful issue, willing sponsor/workflow owner, one improvement can be bounded responsibly, and the tools/access/rules/tests can be confirmed. Fit for Path B: the problem is real, but a responsible one-improvement scope is still unclear or the owner wants a broader review across all seven areas. Not fit: explain why without pressure. A prospect may legitimately want information or more time.

Common responses: “We already have a provider” → ask what works and whether they can solve this. “Can you guarantee savings?” → promise a defined deliverable and tests, not a business outcome outside your control. “What does it cost?” → answer with the current public options: a free working session, Path A at $2,499 when one improvement is clear, or Path B at $999 when broader review is needed. “I need to think” → ask what remains unclear and whether a follow-up is wanted.

Recap draft: “Thanks for walking through [process]. We focused on [area] and your question about [problem]. I observed [facts], [small fix] [was/was not] made, and [unknown] still needs checking. The next best route is [Path A / Path B / no build] because [reason]. Please correct anything I missed.” Review before sending.

Advance gate: the owner has the summary, understands the next route, and either (a) approves a build scope conversation, or (b) wants the paid Business Improvement Plan. Failure path: no free-plan-by-email workaround; clarify, pause or close. No answer is not an acceptance.

5. Diagnose: conduct the Business Improvement Plan

Trigger: approved discovery scope and agreed payment milestone confirmed. Inputs: client goals, named participants, access/data permissions, agreed areas/timeframe and discovery acceptance criteria. Owner: Evan; client workflow owner validates facts. Output: an evidence pack feeding the written plan. This is not an unlimited audit of the entire company.

Agree the diagnostic boundary before starting

Complete: client ID; business goals; processes/locations in depth; people/interview count or other practical limit; observation/sample period; permitted systems/data; meetings and walkthrough; deliverable format; due date/dependencies; exclusions; fee/credit; review/correction process; cancellation/rescheduling terms. Name who can approve extra discovery. A business-wide opportunity screen can be broad; deep investigation remains bounded.[1]

Seven-area review worksheet

Review all seven areas at a high level before choosing the one improvement that deserves a full blueprint. Measure only from records that already exist. When a measure is missing, record the gap and the lightest credible way to collect it.

  • Offer review: what is sold, to whom, and how pricing/packaging is decided. Measure sales by service, average job value and where prices are set only if those records already exist.
  • Marketing review: how people find the business and what prompts an enquiry. Measure enquiries by source and time to first reply only where the source and timing records already exist.
  • Sales review: how enquiries become quotes, bookings or signed work. Measure quote turnaround, quotes awaiting reply and enquiry-to-booking rate only where those records already exist.
  • Delivery and customer experience review: how the work is fulfilled and how customers are updated. Measure time to first contact, missed updates and repeat problems only where those records already exist.
  • Retention review: how customers come back, refer others and leave reviews. Measure repeat-customer rate, review requests sent and referrals only where those records already exist.
  • Operations and people review: schedules, tools, information and handoffs between people. Measure re-keyed entries, admin hours and handoff delays only where those records already exist.
  • Finance review: invoicing, collecting payment and seeing costs clearly. Measure days to invoice, days to collect and overdue balances only where those records already exist.

Discovery question bank

Use the relevant questions, not an interrogation script:

  • Goals and customers: What should be different in 30–90 days? Which customers or staff notice the issue? What must not change?
  • Work as done: Show one recent ordinary case, one difficult case and one failure. What starts work, what finishes it, who owns each handoff, and which rules require judgment?
  • Systems and data: What is the source of truth? Which fields are essential? Who owns access? Are APIs/export/import available under the current plan? What are the limits, renewal dates and vendor constraints? Verify rather than assume compatibility.
  • Volume and timing: Cases per period; peak periods; deadlines; queue age; working hours; seasonal differences; duplicate/retry behavior. What does a quiet day look like?
  • Cost and consequences: Hands-on minutes per case, rework, waiting, error frequency, missed commitments and customer complaints. Which numbers can be observed? Which are estimates?
  • People and adoption: Who will use a change? What will they stop doing? What training or approval is needed? Who covers absences? What would make them bypass it?
  • Risk and permissions: Personal/sensitive data? Financial, safety, health, employment or legal consequence? External messages or money movement? What must always be reviewed by a human? What happens if the result is wrong or absent?
  • Alternatives and economics: Can the existing tool, vendor, simpler policy or staff training solve it? What does each option cost in client effort, licenses, maintenance and switching—not just Jax fees?

Evidence and baseline worksheet

For each finding record: finding ID; process step; evidence reference/date; source type (observed / client estimate / hypothesis); sample and period; affected owner; frequency; consequence; confidence; alternative explanation; unanswered question. Do not turn an estimate into a measured fact during summarization.

Baseline record: metric name; business meaning; unit; numerator/denominator where relevant; eligible population/exclusions; time zone; sample/period; extraction method; source/owner; quality gaps. If absent, prescribe a collection method and first review date; do not invent a baseline or block a useful measurement-only first step.

Conservative benefit model: cases/month × minutes of hands-on work actually avoided ÷ 60 = potential capacity hours released. Subtract review, exceptions and upkeep. Multiply by an agreed loaded labor cost only to estimate capacity value—not automatic payroll savings. Separate additional bookings, revenue and contribution; avoid counting the same gain as both time savings and revenue. Show low/base/high assumptions, client effort, implementation/support/provider costs and what would falsify the case. The consulting source supports building the situation, objectives and accountability around client inputs; its enterprise prices and ROI examples are not Jax benchmarks.[6]

Advance gate: enough evidence to compare options and bound the first recommendation, with client factual corrections resolved or explicitly listed. Failure path: request missing evidence within scope, recommend a measurement period, or renegotiate scope/date before extra work. If there is no sensible build, deliver an honest plan recommending process/native-tool change or no build.

6. Deliver the Business Improvement Plan

Trigger: discovery evidence is ready for synthesis. Inputs: current-state evidence, options, baseline or collection plan, constraints and verified feasibility. Owner: Evan authors; client sponsor/workflow owner reviews facts and chooses what to do. Output: a client-owned seven-area review, ranked opportunities, measurement plan and one detailed implementation blueprint, with an optional fixed-price quote only when the client wants Jax Systems to build and feasibility is proven.[1]

Reusable plan structure

  1. Decision brief: goal, current problem, evidence strength, recommended starting point, expected benefit as a hypothesis, key risks and the decision requested. State if no build is justified.
  2. Seven-area high-level review: offer, marketing, sales, delivery and customer experience, retention, operations and people, and finance. For each area, record what was observed, what records exist, what is still unknown and what can be measured now without inventing numbers.
  3. Ranked opportunities: include do nothing, simplify policy/process, configure existing tools, training and a new build where appropriate. Name why one improvement should come first and why others are later, optional or not recommended.
  4. Measurement plan: available baseline, missing-data collection plan, proposed target, observation period, method/owner and counter-metrics such as error rate or review burden. Targets are not guarantees.[1]
  5. One detailed implementation blueprint: the single recommended improvement in enough detail for the client to execute or hand off. Cover the outcome/end state, native-tool alternative checked first, tools/accounts, steps, rules, dependencies, data/security/adoption controls, tests, failure handling, maintenance expectation, client effort and ownership.
  6. Optional fixed-price quote: include this only if the client wants Jax Systems to build and feasibility is proven. State deliverables, exclusions, acceptance checks, prerequisites, price/credit arithmetic, provider costs, target timing, owners and proposal validity. If feasibility is still unproven, say what must be verified before quoting.
  7. Appendix: evidence index, assumptions, unresolved questions, alternative options and operating/support implications. Do not embed raw secrets or unnecessary personal records.

Opportunity-card template: opportunity ID; recognizable problem; evidence/confidence; baseline or collection plan; proposed change/native alternative; likely benefit and assumptions; effort/dependencies; risk; adoption owner; recurring costs/support burden; measure; priority/reason; next decision.

Use high / medium / low judgments with a reason, not pseudo-precise ROI scores. First apply hard filters: permission, unacceptable harm, missing owner, no fallback, unsupported integration or inadequate coverage. Only then rank by credible benefit, frequency, feasibility, adoption and total cost. A potentially valuable high-risk idea can be “later—not suitable for a first build.”

Walkthrough and acceptance

Send the plan through the agreed private channel. Walk through facts first, alternatives next, recommendation and scope last. Ask the client to correct errors, state whether priorities match, and name unresolved decisions. Record version, delivery date, attendees, corrections and next action. Plan acceptance means the agreed diagnostic work was delivered; it does not mean the client agrees to build, agrees every assumption is true, or waives defects.

The client can use the blueprint internally without Jax Systems or hand it to another provider. Do not hold it back to force a build decision.

Advance gate: all agreed plan components delivered, factual feedback handled under agreed review terms, and client elects to consider a build or keep the blueprint for internal use. Failure path: correct missing/incorrect agreed work, record disputed evidence, or deliver the justified no-build conclusion. Do not withhold the plan as a sales lever or describe unused build credit as a refund entitlement unless the agreement says so.

7. Sell and scope the build

Trigger: client wants a recommended improvement. Inputs: free-session summary and direct-scope evidence, or approved plan blueprint and feasibility evidence, plus delivery capacity and approved pricing. Owner: Evan for quote; authorized client signatory for purchase. Output: a bounded Path A scope card or build agreement from Path A or Path B, not “we'll automate your business.”

Scope card / statement-of-work inputs

  • Trigger, current pain and measured baseline, with observed facts separated from estimates.
  • End state and exact deliverables: configuration/code, test evidence, documentation, training, handover and any stabilization period.
  • Named process, location(s), users/roles, tools/accounts and integration permissions.
  • Rules/branches, input/schema, output, volume/peak and timing assumptions.
  • Explicit exclusions: extra locations/tools/channels, data migration, historical cleanup, new capabilities, ongoing operations and regulated/high-risk decisions unless specifically agreed.
  • Acceptance tests, expected results, named reviewer, review period, defect handling and sign-off method.
  • Workflow owner plus client responsibilities for access, sample data, decisions, user availability, content approval and manual fallback.
  • Milestones/dependencies, target dates, delay/pause/restart process, change control and cancellation.
  • Fixed price; plan credit once where applicable; remaining balance; payment milestones/due dates; provider charges/taxes where applicable; licensing/IP and account ownership.
  • Warranty/defect-remedy boundary, optional care scope, liability/privacy/security terms and exit/handover. Have actual agreements reviewed by appropriate counsel. This manual is not a contract template certified for use.

Pricing logic and commercial gate

Check the work against standard-build assumptions; do not price solely by tool count. Include discovery already performed, implementation, testing, project communication, training, launch/stabilization, likely exceptions and provider costs. Estimate internal effort for capacity/economics while retaining a fixed customer price. If it cannot be delivered responsibly at the standard price, narrow it or quote a different scope—not hidden future care charges.

For the standard example: if one improvement is already clear from the free working session, Path A is $2,499 with no plan fee paid first. If Path B happened first, the plan paid is $999; total including plan stays $2,499; remaining build balance is $1,500; optional care is $499/month; provider fees stay separate.[1] A proposed payment schedule must be written and accepted before work; this manual does not invent a deposit percentage, late fee, refund promise, credit expiry, defect warranty or deemed-acceptance rule. Resolve those in the live terms.

Quote register: version/date; scope ID; price; credit already applied; remaining balance; milestone amounts/dates; taxes/provider fees; validity; customer signatory; approval evidence; invoice/payment status. A sent invoice is not collected revenue.

Repeat-price eligibility record: Evan checks and records the existing connection/configuration IDs, reusable tested delivery pattern, unchanged security boundary, named extra outcome, volume/rule differences, integration/location/data-migration needs, new acceptance tests and estimated support load. The client confirms scope and price. New tools, locations, data movement or materially greater risk/complexity require a separate quote; do not presume eligibility. The $250 additional care rate needs its own reuse/support assessment and a live extra improvement. Record the rationale and approval in the quote register.

Change control—use it before doing the work

Classify a request against the signed scope: defect (fails agreed behavior), included adjustment (same outcome within written allowance), new scope (new outcome, integration, location, data movement or wider rules), or uncertain (needs review). Do not charge a new-build fee to hide an in-scope defect; do not treat every small-sounding request as free.

Change-request template: ID/date/requestor; current scope/version; requested change and reason; category with rationale; impact on cost/date/security/acceptance/support; options (defer / replace scope / separate quote); approval by both authorized parties; revised scope and baseline. Keep original work moving only if safe and independent; no new work or billing until the change is agreed.

Advance gate: signed agreement, client permissions, feasible delivery slot, commercial details settled and required payment milestone confirmed. Failure path: clarify, reduce scope, defer or decline; never start a build on enthusiasm alone.

8. Onboard: accounts, data and responsibilities

Trigger: build gate passed. Inputs: agreement, approved scope/tests, client owners, permitted data and system inventory. Owner: Evan coordinates; client administrator grants access. Output: kickoff record, secure access inventory, delivery board, communication plan and approved data flow.

Kickoff agenda and access checklist

Confirm the goal, scope/exclusions, dates/dependencies, payment state, working participants, approvers, status cadence, support contact path and escalation. Check holidays, absence and the client's manual capacity; do not invent a backup human. Ask the actual users to demonstrate ordinary work before replacing it.

  • Use client-owned accounts/projects and named invitations or service identities, least privilege, MFA and read-only access until writes are needed and authorized. Separate client environments and credentials with actual access controls; a folder name is not tenant isolation.
  • Put secrets in an approved vault/secrets manager, not notes, tickets, repositories, screenshots, prompts or this page. Record only a pointer, scope, owner and expiry/revocation process. OWASP recommends least-privilege access and centralized, auditable secret lifecycle management.[9]
  • Prefer synthetic/anonymized test data. Before real data, document categories, purpose, authorized people, storage/region, approved vendors/subprocessors/model provider, retention, deletion, backups and incident contacts. “AI has access” is not a data-processing agreement.
  • Check vendor terms, permitted API usage and client subscription limits. If protected health, payment-card, legal, employee or other sensitive/high-consequence data exceeds capability or contract coverage, stop and obtain appropriate specialist review.
  • Confirm logging is useful but minimized/redacted; restrict access to logs and exports. Keep client data out of reusable shared examples and public artifacts.
  • Establish who can pause, approve production writes, reconnect OAuth, rotate credentials, restore backups and authorize data deletion. Retest access after changes.

Access-inventory template: system/project; business owner; administrator; identity/service account; permission scope; environment; credential vault pointer (not value); grant/expiry/review dates; data categories; renewal/provider billing owner; revocation/rotation steps; dependencies; export/backup location pointer.

Communication compact: primary private channel; who checks it; normal working coverage/time zone; status-update day; emergency reporting route; client's operational fallback contact; planned absences/backup arrangement; approval method. A messaging app being installed is not a staffed help desk.

Advance gate: least-privilege access verified, data path and responsibilities approved, acceptance reviewer available, fallback possible. Failure path: do not build around missing access with shared passwords or unauthorized scraping; adjust schedule, use sanitized examples or rescope.

9. Implement: observe, build, prove, prepare

Trigger: onboarding gate passed. Inputs: scope, representative permitted examples, acceptance plan, approved environment and rollback design. Owner: Evan; client workflow owner checks rules. Output: versioned release candidate with evidence and an operating guide.

Delivery sequence

  1. Observe and confirm: validate source-of-truth fields/rules, native alternatives and smallest useful slice. Reopen discovery for materially new uncertainty; do not silently grow the job.
  2. Design the safe path: map input → validation → processing → human approval where required → output → reconciliation. Define idempotency, timeouts, bounded retries/backoff, cost/volume limits, queue and recovery. An uncertain external write must be reconciled before retrying; never send the same message twice because the acknowledgement was lost.
  3. Build in staging/sandbox: version code/configuration/prompts and dependencies, separate secrets, keep a change log and pin the candidate release. Test permission boundaries and client separation. Avoid unreviewed model/tool auto-upgrades.
  4. Prove behavior: run the test matrix below with preserved expected versus actual results. Independently inspect consequential outputs; use a reviewer where risk warrants. A second model's opinion is not proof of correctness.
  5. Prepare operations: implement health checks, alert ownership, manual fallback, restore/rollback, client training and actual handover. Measure founder/reviewer time and vendor cost, not just model tokens.

If AI is used: source content is untrusted data, not instructions; validate outputs with deterministic rules where possible, constrain available actions and require human approval for high-risk operations. OWASP notes that RAG/fine-tuning do not fully mitigate prompt injection and recommends least privilege, external-content separation and human approval for high-risk actions.[8] Test malicious instructions inside retrieved documents, unsupported claims, incorrect extraction, missing context, refusals and provider/model changes. “The prompt says don't” is not an access control.

Test matrix / evidence record

Case Required behavior to define and test
Ordinary work Correct end-to-end output reaches the real intended destination
Missing/malformed input Reject or quarantine, explain, no unsafe write
Duplicate/replayed trigger One intended business action, stable deduplication key
Partial/uncertain success Check destination before replay; reconcile rather than guess
Expired access / permission denied Stop affected action, alert the right owner, no token leakage
Timeout / vendor outage / rate limit Bounded retry, durable pending state, fallback, no runaway cost
Wrong output despite “success” Quality/business invariant detects mismatch or routes to review
Wrong client/recipient Access/recipient boundary blocks; no cross-client disclosure
Peak volume / backlog Known limit, queue ageing alarm, no silent discarded work
Missed run / monitor failure Independent freshness/watchdog check signals missing evidence
Pause / rollback / restore Named operator can stop; restore tested against agreed recovery needs
Reconnect / resume Reauthorize safely, reconcile pending work, no duplicates

Test-record template: test ID; requirement/risk; release/config version; permitted fixture; steps; expected result; actual result; destination evidence; pass/fail; reviewer/date; defect and retest link. No customer messaging or destructive production test without permission.

Weekly status draft: completed and evidenced; next; decisions needed and due dates; blockers/impact; scope changes; costs/capacity risks; next demonstration. Do not say “90% complete” when acceptance and failure cases remain unknown.

Advance gate: agreed tests passed, critical defects resolved, client can review, rollback/fallback and monitoring evidence ready. Failure path: isolate defect, record impact, fix/retest; if the discovered requirement changes scope, use section 7. Do not relabel a failed test as an accepted exception without authorized risk review.

10. Accept, launch and hand over

Trigger: tested candidate ready. Inputs: test evidence, client reviewer, release version, cutover/rollback plan, operating guide and support choice. Owner: client acceptance owner approves; Evan executes authorized cutover. Output: accepted live release, trained users and a receipt of handover.

Launch gate—read aloud before switching

  • [ ] Signed scope/tests still match the candidate; no unauthorized additions.
  • [ ] Agreed normal/error/repeat/security tests have evidence; no unresolved critical defect.
  • [ ] Baseline exists or the client accepts a specific collection plan; no fabricated savings claim.
  • [ ] Backup/export and restore tested where applicable; recovery objectives and data-loss tolerance agreed for this workflow. A successful backup job is not a restore test.
  • [ ] Client workflow owner can use the system, pause it and perform manual fallback.
  • [ ] Alert delivery, named recipient, escalation and independent missing-evidence check tested.
  • [ ] Client approves cutover window and any production write; rollback triggers and authority clear.
  • [ ] Support is separately accepted or declined, with responsibility after handover explicit.

No-care minimum: every unattended build still needs a tested pause/manual route, failure visibility and an independent missing-outcome check proportionate to its risk. For a handoff-only project, transfer those controls and alert ownership to a named, trained client operator before stabilization ends; verify actual receipt and acknowledgement. Jax provides no continuing human monitoring after the agreed responsibility ends. If the client cannot operate the necessary controls and declines suitable support, redesign for supervised use or do not launch unattended.

Hard authority gate: before unattended production, document who may pause unsafe actions, invoke fallback, reauthorize access and approve replay/resumption, including outside Jax coverage. The client must authorize a safe stop mechanism in advance. No reachable authorized owner or safe stop path means no unattended launch; an incident is not the time to discover this.

Cutover and stabilization

Snapshot/export where appropriate; record old/new versions and start time; stop conflicting old schedules; release the approved version; run an authorized smoke transaction; inspect actual output/destination; reconcile pending work. Shadow or limited rollout when appropriate, with a written observation duration and success/rollback criteria—not an automatic two-week promise for every project.

Rollback on unsafe output, data exposure, duplicated action, uncontrolled cost or failed agreed critical test. Pause writes, preserve evidence, invoke client fallback, restore the known-good version only when that is safe, and reconcile affected records. Restoring code does not undo messages already sent or corrupted data; plan compensation separately with authorization.

Define stabilization start/end, owner, included defect fixes, check frequency and exit criteria in the scope. It is not free indefinite care. After final deployment, repeat critical smoke and alert checks on the actual production environment; a working preview is not proof of working production.

Acceptance receipt: scope/release; test-result links; client reviewer/date/decision; remaining noncritical issues with owner/date and explicit agreement; production verification; training/handover receipt; stabilization end; care choice/start; invoice milestone. No assumed acceptance from silence unless counsel-reviewed signed terms explicitly provide for it.

Handover pack: system purpose/limits; architecture/data flow; client account/admin inventory; source/configuration and license ownership; ordinary operation; approvals; errors/queue replay; pause/rollback/restore steps; monitor/alert definitions; dependency/renewal costs; support/exit route; known limitations. User demonstrates key tasks back to Evan. Ownership/licensing exceptions must already be agreed, not revealed at exit.

Advance gate: client acceptance recorded, actual production verified, handover complete and any active care arrangement confirmed. Failure path: keep or restore the safe/manual route, own defect remediation under the agreement, and do not declare “live and supported” from a demo alone.

11. Operate: monitor the business result, not just the cron

Trigger: a system goes live under an agreed operating/support responsibility. Inputs: system inventory, expected work, outcome definitions, check cadence, privacy limits and responder coverage. Owner: named operator; Evan only where contracted. Output: timestamped health evidence, exception queue and actionable alerts. No monitoring is installed by this manual.

Google SRE distinguishes symptoms from causes and notes that end-to-end tests are needed to detect a system serving the wrong content even when basic requests succeed.[5] Jax's adaptation: a run can succeed while the customer's work fails. A green schedule is one piece of evidence, not the verdict.

Six layers of health

Layer What to check Failure that a cron exit misses
Execution Expected start/finish, duration, last success; independent heartbeat Scheduler stopped, host asleep, monitor itself absent
Inputs Source freshness, eligible work count, schema, credentials, vendor availability Empty extract, expired OAuth, renamed field
Processing Validation failures, retries, queue age, duplicates, error rate Stuck item, repeated action, partial success
Destination Read-back/receipt linked by correlation ID and correct tenant/recipient API accepted but record/message never arrived
Business quality Completeness, timeliness, accuracy, rule invariants, human sample/review Wrong amount, missing case, irrelevant AI output
Economics / dependencies Usage, spend, limits, expiring access/licenses, vendor/model change Retry loop cost, quota exhaustion, retired API/model

Use both internal telemetry and an external/synthetic check appropriate to the risk. Keep synthetics in controlled destinations so they cannot create fake customer activity or trigger billing. Every eligible item should have an auditable state: pending, succeeded with evidence, quarantined, explicitly excluded, or failed with an owner. Reconcile counts by business ID and time window, not just aggregate totals.

Monitoring-contract template—required per live system

System/client ID; business owner; operator; critical hours/time zone; expected trigger/cadence; eligible inputs; good outcome and deadline; metric source; detection method/independence; check interval; grace period; thresholds; excluded/planned-maintenance rules; alert destination; acknowledgement/escalation route; manual fallback; recovery/data-loss objectives; replay policy; retention/privacy; spend limits; who reviews changes; last tested/next review.

  • SLI: what actually happened, e.g. eligible cases with complete, correct output before deadline ÷ all eligible cases. Define exclusions before counting.
  • SLO: an internal or agreed target over a named window, not an unlimited guarantee.
  • SLA: only a specific contractual commitment and remedy, if offered. A dashboard target is not automatically an SLA.
  • Detection time, acknowledgement time and restoration time are different. A five-minute poll cannot promise a five-minute human fix. Include check interval, job/runtime, grace, notification path and coverage in the detection/response model.

For sparse SMB workflows, inspect absolute missed/late counts, age and known expected activity alongside rates. Zero work can mean a quiet day or broken ingestion. Google discusses low-traffic sensitivity and the need for alert precision, recall and detection-time trade-offs; enterprise burn-rate defaults should not be copied blindly.[7]

Worked monitoring design—illustrative only

For a daily internal quote-follow-up task list: it is due by 08:15 America/New_York on client working days. Independent check at 08:20 compares eligible source IDs with task IDs, correct owner/date and current completion status; zero expected cases is explicitly recorded. These are demonstration parameters, not Jax service promises.

A run at 08:00 exiting zero is not success if a source case was omitted. Alert on any missing eligible case, unverified destination or stale source; suppress planned closed days using the agreed calendar. Include daylight-saving/time-zone tests. A human quality sample catches issues the deterministic reconciliation cannot. Decide sample size/frequency and reviewer in the engagement, based on risk.

Alert envelope: client/system; observed symptom and impact; severity; first/last seen and time zone; expected versus actual; correlation IDs; last good output/version; safe evidence link; current fallback; owner; next action; next update. Never include tokens or full sensitive records in a notification. Deduplicate repeated alerts into one incident; do not suppress new scope/impact just because one alert is open.

Advance gate: health is supported by fresh evidence, exceptions owned and alert path tested. Failure path: stale/missing evidence means unknown, not green. Trigger incident triage or a review ticket; use the fallback if safe completion cannot be established. If the required risk/coverage exceeds capacity, redesign or decline the workflow.

12. When something breaks: incident response

Trigger: alert, client report, missing outcome, unsafe output or suspected data/security issue. Inputs: symptom/evidence, system inventory, agreed support terms and client contacts. Owner: named incident lead; Evan only within actual authority/coverage. Output: contained impact, verified recovery, client communication and corrective action. NIST's current incident-response guidance integrates preparation, detection, response and recovery into ongoing risk management.[10]

Severity and coverage—proposal, not a signed SLA

Severity Example / decision Proposed handling during agreed coverage
SEV1 critical Suspected exposure, corrupt/unauthorized output, uncontrolled spend, or essential work stopped with no safe workaround Stop unsafe action under prior authority, urgently contact the designated client lead and incident lead; invoke specialist/legal/security route as appropriate
SEV2 major Agreed business deadline at risk, partial outage or repeated wrong output; safe workaround exists Triage promptly, activate workaround, agree updates and recovery priority
SEV3 routine Noncritical defect, isolated exception, cosmetic issue or improvement request Log, classify scope and schedule under ordinary support/change terms

Do not sell a 24/7 SEV1 promise while Evan has employment, sleep, holidays and no staffed backup. Before care starts, fill exact coverage days/hours/time zone, acknowledgement targets by severity, outside-coverage behavior, update cadence, fallback/backup, escalation contacts and commercial remedies if any. Distinguish a response target from a resolution promise. If those fields are blank, support is not ready to sell. For a critical workflow needing faster recovery than available staffing, reduce autonomy, add qualified coverage with approval, or decline it.

Runbook: stabilize first, diagnose second

  1. Open and acknowledge: assign incident ID/lead, timestamp and severity from business impact, affected clients/processes and known versus unknown facts. Confirm who is actually responding; a notification is not acknowledgement.
  2. Contain safely: pause affected writes/sends or bounded component under pre-agreed authority; use the client manual route. If authority is absent, contact the authorized owner. Preserve relevant redacted logs and state; do not erase evidence during a suspected security incident.
  3. Assess scope: last known good, pending/sent/failed cases, impacted dates/recipients, recent changes, access failures, provider status and cost. Suspected data exposure goes to the client's security/legal/insurer contacts as appropriate; do not improvise breach-notification law or promise there was no exposure.
  4. Diagnose: compare live release/configuration with intended version; check source and destination separately; inspect expired credentials, schema/model changes, permissions, rate limits, scheduling and silent bad outputs. Do not repeatedly rerun the entire job.
  5. Recover: reauthorize through the secure process, repair or roll back, test with controlled data, read back real destinations, reconcile affected IDs and obtain approval for replay/compensation. Check duplicates before replay; cap retries/spend.
  6. Verify and reopen: business outcome restored, affected backlog accounted for, client owner validates, monitors/alerts still work, and a defined observation period passes. Decide who may resume automation. “Service restarted” is not recovery evidence.
  7. Close and learn: timeline, impact with evidence/unknowns, cause or current best explanation, action taken, recovery proof, client notice and prevention owner/date. Add a regression, monitor or runbook change; review recurrence and support economics.

Initial message draft: “We identified [observed issue] at [time/time zone]. [Known scope] is affected; [uncertainty] is still being checked. We have [paused action / activated workaround]. Please [specific client action if needed]. [Named owner] is investigating; our next update is [agreed time]. We are not yet able to confirm a restoration time.” No speculative blame or false reassurance.

Incident-record template: ID/system; reporter/detection; severity/impact; lead/client contact; timeline; evidence; scope/affected IDs; containment authority/action; workaround; last good version; hypotheses/tests; recovery/replay approvals; destination validation; communications; follow-up owner/date; billable/in-scope decision after containment. Do not negotiate a new project while an authorized safety action waits, but do not assume unlimited unpaid repair beyond agreed responsibilities.

Advance gate: outcome restored and reconciled, client informed, prevention owned. Failure path: continue safe fallback/escalation and honest updates; do not close from a green heartbeat or exhausted attention.

13. Care, maintenance and controlled improvement

Trigger: optional care agreement begins after launch, or a contracted review/maintenance date arrives. Inputs: supported-system register, scope/coverage, metrics, incident/change history, client dependencies and allowance. Owner: agreed care owner and client workflow owner. Output: reliable operations within a bounded service and an evidence-based review.

The published starting point is $499/month for optional support of the first agreed improvement, with small-change allowance and support expectations agreed before care starts; it is not unlimited support or a new-build allowance.[1] Define: exact supported systems/versions; monitoring and check frequency; response coverage; included defect investigation; small-change examples and allowance (hours, count or another explicit bound); carry-over or expiry; third-party fees/usage caps; exclusions; renewal/cancellation; dependency obligations; access/exit; start/end of billing. Do not assume these terms from a price alone.

Proposed operating cadence—right-size it per system

  • At each run / event: validation, bounded retries, outcome evidence and exception state where the design supports it.
  • Each working day: review unresolved alerts/queue age and expected outcomes for systems needing it. Low-risk batch systems may have a different agreed cadence; critical ones may need more coverage than Jax offers.
  • Weekly: inspect recurring failures, source/destination reconciliation and quality samples; review costs, access expiry, upcoming vendor changes and open client decisions.
  • Monthly: compare actual outcomes/cost/adoption with baseline, review incidents and allowance used, inspect dependency versions/permissions, and agree the next review. Revisit whether care remains useful.
  • Before a material change: impact analysis, client authorization as required, staging/regression, rollback and post-release check. Test backup restore, fallback and access revocation at an agreed risk-based interval and after material architecture changes—not just when an outage occurs.

Maintenance is not blind auto-update. Track vendor/model/API deprecations, credential expiry, subscription renewals, workflow rule changes and upstream schema changes. Pin where practical, schedule testing and plan migration or a fresh quote if necessary. A silent model swap can change outputs even if the transport still works.

Change classification: changing a wording/template within the same approved process may fit the allowance; a new channel, location, integration, data migration or business capability normally needs a new quote. A vendor breaking an existing connection requires investigation under the care terms; a major migration may be new scope depending on the agreement. Decide from the contract, not a blanket rule.

Advance gate: included work/cost is visible, outcomes measured honestly and new work separately agreed. Failure path: if repeated exceptions make the system unreliable or uneconomic, propose redesign/repricing, reduce risk or exit cleanly; do not conceal degraded service behind a monthly status page.

14. Manage the relationship, records and money

Trigger: any decision, meeting, delivery, incident, invoice or scheduled review. Inputs: primary evidence and actual agreements. Owner: Evan maintains one private client record; client approves its decisions. Output: a current source of truth with a next action and no orphaned commitments. The consulting playbook supplies a commercial lifecycle framework, not proof that Jax already has these operations connected.[2]

Private client record—minimum schema

Use stable client, engagement, system, change and incident IDs. Link, rather than duplicate, source documents. A restricted folder plus a small index can be the first implementation if access, backup and retrieval work; no CRM purchase is required by this manual. Real records do not belong on GitHub Pages, in unlisted gists or in browser-only state on a public page.

  • Relationship: approved contacts/roles, preferred channel, sponsor/workflow owner/admin, consent/recording preferences, enquiry source, stage, owner, next action/date.
  • Commercial: discovery/build/care agreements and versions, scope/acceptance, quote/credit, invoices/due dates/receipts, renewal/cancellation and ownership/licensing.
  • Delivery: plan/evidence, baseline/measurement design, dependencies, decisions, changes, tests/releases, training and signed acceptance.
  • Systems/access: client-owned accounts, administrator and credential pointers, approved data flow, vendor/renewal inventory, retention and exit dependencies.
  • Operations: monitoring contract, support terms, runbook, incidents/changes, cost/usage, outcome reviews and next review date.
  • Closure: access revocation, asset/data export receipt, remaining licenses/billing, deletion schedule/holds and final acceptance of handover.

Every open item needs an owner, due date, risk and next action. Keep access limited, backups tested, retention intentional and history versioned. AI-generated summaries must be checked against originals before becoming commitments.

Minimum record procedure before the first engagement

Evan selects an access-controlled, client-approved storage service and creates one restricted client folder with an index and subfolders for agreements, discovery, delivery/tests, operations/incidents and closure. Use a separate approved secrets vault; store pointers only. In the index, create the stable IDs, role/contact register, stage/gate evidence, open-item table and next review date. Before use, test authorized access, denial for an unrelated identity, recovery from a versioned backup, and retrieval of a sample evidence link. Record actual locations and backup/retention owner privately.

Update the index after each consequential event and before every handoff; preserve approved versions rather than overwriting history. On each working day with active work, review due/blocked items; weekly, check missing evidence, access and backup status. At closure, reconcile the exit checklist, record retention/deletion dates and restrict the archive. This is an implementation procedure, not a claim that a store was provisioned. Without a chosen, tested home, do not accept client data or sell paid discovery.

Invoice and overdue handling

Prepare invoices from the signed milestone and credit record; verify legal entity/payment instructions independently, especially any changed bank details. Reconcile payment receipt before marking collected or unlocking a payment gate. Track invoice number, engagement/milestone, total/credit/tax, issued/due/paid dates, outstanding amount and next follow-up. Do not collect card numbers in notes or messages; use the agreed secure payment mechanism.

For overdue amounts: check for error or dispute, contact the authorized person privately, document the response and follow the agreed reminder/suspension process. Never invent a late fee, shut off a client system abruptly or withhold their agreed assets without contractual/legal grounds. Review safe continuity and access before any suspension.

Monthly value-review page / meeting

  1. Reporting window and what the system is meant to achieve.
  2. Eligible work, completed/late/failed/excluded counts and data-quality gaps.
  3. Business metric versus baseline, with comparable periods and caveats; client usage/adoption and actual feedback.
  4. Incidents, manual exceptions, changes, cost/provider charges and allowance used.
  5. What improved, what did not, unknowns and action owners/dates.
  6. Continue as-is / simplify / stop / quote another bounded improvement; next review.

Time released is capacity, not automatically cash saved; before/after movement is not proof Jax caused it. Ask for referral, logo, quote or case study only with separate explicit permission and an accurate account of results. No obligatory testimonial as a condition of routine support.

Advance gate: record reconciled, commitments owned, client understands results/cost and next step. Failure path: correct discrepancies, surface dissatisfaction, assign resolution and revisit value before proposing expansion.

15. Expand, renew or exit cleanly

Trigger: new opportunity, renewal/review, cancellation, relationship end or unsafe/unsupported workflow. Inputs: agreements, actual results, open work, systems/access inventory and retention obligations. Owner: Evan and authorized client owner. Output: a new signed scope, explicit renewal/continuation, or a documented clean exit.

Expansion: return to diagnosis, verify that the next improvement is useful and capacity exists. Reuse patterns, not another client's data. Apply eligible repeat-build/support pricing only after verifying reuse and scope; do not bill $250/month for each internal technical step.[1] A client may choose a different vendor or take over operations.

Exit checklist / closure certificate

  • [ ] Confirm effective date, notice/cancellation terms, outstanding work, payment disputes and who will operate after exit.
  • [ ] Inventory code/configuration, prompts, documentation, datasets, access, hosting, domains, licenses, schedules, alerts and third-party billing.
  • [ ] Export/transfer agreed client-owned materials through a secure channel; verify the client can open/import them and access administrator accounts. Record receipt, not just “file sent.”
  • [ ] Explain dependencies/costs that continue, licensed components and any agreed ownership exceptions. Transfer billing where agreed; do not cancel an account the client still needs.
  • [ ] Transfer or safely stop schedules, webhooks, queues and notifications; reconcile pending work; prove there are no ghost jobs or duplicate triggers.
  • [ ] Revoke Jax personnel/service identities and tokens; rotate shared secrets where unavoidable; check revocation at the actual provider. OWASP notes that restarting an application does not revoke stolen credentials; the backing service must expire or revoke them.[9]
  • [ ] Apply the agreed retention/deletion schedule, legal/contractual holds and backup expiry; restrict retained records and document dates/exceptions. Do not promise deletion from immutable backups or vendor systems without verifying their capabilities.
  • [ ] Final report: end date, handover receipt, stopped/transferred services, access revocation evidence, continuing owner/costs, open exceptions and deletion/retention obligations.

Never use client-hosting dependency as lock-in. Canceling Jax care does not itself cancel the client's vendor accounts or guarantee software keeps working without maintenance. Make both facts clear.

Advance gate: handover, safe continuity/stop, access, billing and retention responsibilities reconciled with evidence. Failure path: keep closure open with named exceptions; do not claim “all deleted” or “fully transferred” from a checklist alone.

16. Walk one fictional engagement through the whole system

Desk rehearsal only. The business, sample, timeline and numbers below are invented to test the process. No prospect was contacted, customer system connected, message sent, payment collected, monitor installed or production failure injected. The exercise exposes decisions; it is not evidence of delivery readiness.

Scenario: a local service office loses track of quote follow-up

The fictional owner wants a daily internal task list from an existing quoting tool. Customer messages remain human-sent. One office, one source account, one task destination; no payment processing, voice agent or autonomous external outreach. Native-tool configuration is considered first.

  1. Free working session: confirm one real-style case, one question, the workflow owner, the business consequence and the data sensitivity. The owner is present. The session stays in client-owned tools and read-only until a specific change is approved. Fit decision: either the next step is clear enough for Path A, or the broader business context still points to Path B.
  2. Route A — direct build is clear: in the session, the team confirms that the problem is one internal quote-follow-up handoff, the current tool exposes the needed export/API, and one improvement can be bounded responsibly. A safe, reversible small fix may be demonstrated, but no customer message is sent. The written summary recommends Path A, not a mandatory plan-first step.
  3. Route B — broader review is needed: if the office cannot yet confirm the actual source export, destination permissions, owner responsibilities or whether a native report already solves the issue, the recommendation is Path B. The fictional client then pays $999 under agreed terms for the Business Improvement Plan, which remains theirs even if the build is declined.
  4. Diagnostic math (inside Path B): invented sample of 30 eligible quotes in a week shows 6 without an assigned follow-up; label it fictional. Proposed future metric: all eligible quotes accounted for by the daily deadline, with excluded cases explicitly classified. Measure hands-on review time separately; do not extrapolate 6 missed follow-ups into 6 lost sales.
  5. Recommendation: compare native reporting, a manual checklist and a small integration. Before a fixed integration quote, confirm the actual source export/API and destination permissions. If native reporting suffices, recommend it instead. If the integration is still the best option, the plan ranks it and documents the implementation blueprint the client owns.
  6. Build agreement: one internal list; owner/date rules; deduplication by quote ID; eligible versus excluded cases; 08:15 local deadline; test/fallback/handover/stabilization scope. Illustrative arithmetic: direct Path A is $2,499 if it was already clear in the free session; if Path B happened first, $999 has already been paid and $1,500 remains. Payment timing and care terms still need a real signed agreement.
  7. Onboarding/build: named read-only source access, controlled test destination, approved data path. Test normal, missing field, duplicate, revoked access, source empty, wrong task owner and missed schedule. Client owner practices export-and-review fallback.
  8. Scope-change inject: owner asks “Could it text customers too?” Category: new capability/channel and consent/risk requirements. Keep the internal-list scope unchanged; log request, explain cost/date/support impact and obtain separate approval before any SMS work. No free addition and no unapproved message.
  9. Acceptance inject: test run says success but one quote is absent. Gate fails. Expected-ID versus destination-ID reconciliation finds the omission; fix and retest before launch. Scheduler success is explicitly rejected as acceptance evidence.
  10. Production-failure inject: after hypothetical launch, source OAuth is revoked. The next expected result is missing. Under the illustrative design, the independent 08:20 check raises a SEV2 because a manual workaround exists; a security sign of compromise would escalate severity. Client uses the export checklist; no invented 24/7 responder appears.
  11. Recovery: named administrator securely reconnects source; inspect affected quote IDs, check what was already created, replay only missing work with approval and bounded retries, verify destination owner/date/count, then obtain permission to resume. Add credential/freshness evidence and a regression case. Do not call a reconnection alone “fixed.”
  12. Review: report eligible versus accounted-for work, late cases, review burden, cost and adoption. Care is optional; no automatic attachment or fabricated savings. An expansion returns to scope control.
  13. Exit inject: the owner takes over. Export and open the runbook/configuration, transfer job/alert ownership, verify provider-level access revocation and document retained records/backups. Existing vendor charges are the client's separate decision.
  14. Unavailable-founder / broken-alert inject: assume the primary notification fails and Evan is outside agreed coverage. The independent watchdog uses the agreed secondary route to the client operator; if no acknowledgement arrives within the workflow’s agreed safe window, the pre-authorized design pauses consequential actions and exposes pending work for manual handling. No fictional backup employee appears. If neither a reachable client operator nor a safe automatic stop exists, this unattended design fails the launch gate. Test alert receipt and acknowledgement failure separately; a successful send is insufficient.

What this rehearsal reveals

The process has a response for scope growth, silent missing output, expired access, safe fallback and exit. It still needs a chosen private record store; actual APIs/permissions; signed commercial and care terms; real contacts/coverage; working monitoring/alert destinations; real test fixtures; and a measured staging/production drill before a live engagement. Those are operational gates, not reasons to invent tooling or promises in a document.

17. Readiness: what must exist before selling and shipping

One manual does not mean building every automation first. Start manually where it is safe and verifiable; automate only a process that already works and has an exception owner.

Before outbound or paid discovery

  • [ ] Current offer/credit and free-session boundary consistent in actual sales materials; named person handles enquiries/replies.
  • [ ] Private record home selected with access control, backup/retrieval and next-action tracking. No live client data in this reading copy.
  • [ ] Free-session notes, discovery scope, plan structure and factual review process ready.
  • [ ] Actual discovery terms, payment mechanism, cancellation/review/credit rules settled with appropriate legal/accounting input.
  • [ ] Data-permission/recording/retention and capability-risk screening ready.
  • [ ] If outbound is used: provider/legal review, legitimate sender, authentication/reply/delivery tests, verified small list, opt-out/suppression and explicit campaign/budget approval. No need to delay an authorized warm conversation for a mass-mail stack.

Before signing a build

  • [ ] First recommendation feasible and economically bounded; native alternatives assessed.
  • [ ] Scope, exclusions, client responsibilities, fixed price, credit, milestones, acceptance, defect/change/cancellation and ownership terms resolved.
  • [ ] Delivery slot, data/security requirements, safe fallback and required specialist capacity actually available.
  • [ ] Support choice explicit. If care is sold, fill the coverage, response/update targets, allowance, costs, escalation, cancellation and exit schedule before commitment—not after a failure.

  • [ ] Client access/data authorizations verified; release/test record complete.
  • [ ] Real destination, duplicates, incorrect outputs, credential failure, missed runs, cost controls and client separation tested.
  • [ ] Monitoring contract implemented where promised; independent missing-evidence check and alert acknowledgement/escalation exercised.
  • [ ] Client trains back the pause/manual/restore route; realistic recovery needs and coverage match.
  • [ ] Approved cutover, client acceptance, handover and post-deploy verification recorded.

Proposed hardening sprint—activation-relative SMART goal

Produce scope/plan/acceptance/incident/exit records and evidence of actual alert receipt, fallback and reconciliation. Evan reviews on day 10 and decides whether a low-risk real engagement is supportable. This goal is proposed; no sprint, customer deadline, bot or scheduled job is activated here.

Review the manual after the first engagement and first material incident; promote only sanitized lessons into reusable templates.

Remaining owner decisions—not silently selected

The paid-plan choice and published starting prices are verified above.
